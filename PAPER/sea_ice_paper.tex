\abstract{%
Monitoring sea ice type from single-channel Synthetic Aperture Radar~(SAR)
imagery is critical for climate science and polar navigation, yet speckle
noise and radiometric ambiguity across ice types limit conventional pipelines.
To the best of our knowledge, no prior work has jointly addressed pixel-level
segmentation and multi-class ice-type classification on SAR. We present a
proof-of-concept, language-conditioned multimodal framework for this joint
task. A frozen CLIP ViT-L/14 backbone, adapted with rank-8 LoRA adapters
(1.57\,M of 304\,M parameters, 0.52\%), is conditioned on a natural-language
prompt encoded by CLIP's text transformer and fused through cross-attention;
an image-conditioned U-Net decoder predicts ice masks while an MLP head
classifies six ice types, trained jointly with a Focal--Tversky and
class-weighted cross-entropy objective. The published configuration uses a
fixed, class-agnostic prompt for every image, so the reported classification
scores cannot arise from label leakage through the text channel. On a 90-image
held-out split the classifier attains weighted-F1\,=\,0.778 and accuracy
0.833, while segmentation reaches mIoU\,=\,0.351 and cIoU\,=\,0.456. We report
every segmentation result against the degenerate all-foreground baseline
(mIoU\,=\,0.383 on this split): the model exceeds the floor on cumulative IoU,
pixel accuracy, and precision, but not on per-image mIoU or Dice — a ceiling
we attribute to the Otsu-binarised scattering-map supervision. The same
harness shows zero-shot LISA-7B, CLIPSeg, and GeoPixel-7B all falling below
the trivial floor, quantifying the substantial domain gap between optical
foundation models and SAR imagery.}

\keyword{SAR sea ice segmentation; language-conditioned segmentation; CLIP;
LoRA; cross-attention fusion; multimodal learning; remote sensing; cryosphere}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}


The polar cryosphere is changing at a pace that demands near-continuous, all-
weather observation. Sea ice extent has declined at a rate of roughly
13\,\% per decade in September minimum extent over the satellite era
\cite{comiso2012large}, with cascading consequences for global ocean
circulation, albedo feedback, and Arctic ecosystems. Understanding not just
\emph{how much} ice is present but \emph{what kind} — young, first-year,
multi-year, glacially derived — is essential because different ice types carry
radically different structural properties, lifespans, and roles in the climate
system \cite{maslanik2011distribution,petty2018arctic}.

Synthetic Aperture Radar satellites have become the workhorse of operational
sea ice monitoring. Unlike passive optical sensors, SAR functions regardless of
cloud cover and solar illumination, making it uniquely suitable for the polar
night and the notoriously overcast Arctic and Antarctic skies. Yet the
backscatter intensities that SAR measures are governed by surface roughness,
dielectric constants, and incidence geometry in ways that interleave the
signatures of different ice types, producing visually ambiguous images to
both human analysts and automated classifiers. The situation is compounded
in publicly available datasets, where imagery is often delivered as a
single-channel grayscale JPEG — sacrificing the polarimetric diversity that
physically separates ice categories \cite{ulaby1990microwave}.

Conventional approaches to SAR sea ice analysis have leaned on hand-crafted
texture features, thresholding-based segmentation, or supervised convolutional
networks trained on labelled archives \cite{brekke2005sea,leigh2014automated}.
More recent work has transplanted U-Net \cite{ronneberger2015unet} and
ResNet-based pipelines to SAR data, improving detection robustness
\cite{liu2021deep}. However, these efforts address either segmentation
\emph{or} classification in isolation; none that we are aware of integrates
natural-language context as a conditioning signal; and, to the best of our
knowledge, none establishes a public, reproducible combined baseline that a
subsequent study can build on.

Concurrently, the emergence of multimodal foundation models (VLMs) has transformed
image understanding more broadly. CLIP \cite{radford2021learning} demonstrated
that aligning visual and textual representations through contrastive
pretraining unlocks powerful zero-shot and few-shot capabilities. CLIPSeg
\cite{luddecke2022image} extended this to open-vocabulary segmentation without
any pixel-level supervision at train time. Most recently, reasoning-segmentation
systems such as LISA \cite{lai2024lisa} have demonstrated the value of
language-instructed visual understanding for complex segmentation tasks,
coupling LLM decoders with pixel-level output heads to enable
instruction-following mask prediction. However, their applicability to
SAR-based cryospheric monitoring remains largely unexplored, as these models
are pretrained predominantly on natural-image and optical remote-sensing corpora.
A closely related paradigm targets underwater imagery \cite{underwater2024infofusion},
establishing the gIoU/cIoU/Dice metric protocol that we adopt here for direct
comparability.

We emphasise the scope of the present work precisely: our model contains no
large language model and generates no text. It is a \emph{language-conditioned}
multimodal architecture — CLIP text embeddings fused with visual features by
cross-attention — not a reasoning-segmentation system in the LISA sense. We
use the published reasoning-segmentation systems as zero-shot comparison
points, not as architectural peers.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} This paper makes the following contributions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=\arabic*.]

  \item We present, to the best of our knowledge, the \textbf{first reported
        deep-learning framework for simultaneous sea-ice segmentation and
        six-class ice-type classification} from SAR imagery, establishing
        reproducible baselines for this joint task.

  \item We introduce a \textbf{language-conditioned multimodal fusion
        architecture} that combines SAR observations with textual prompts
        through cross-attention.

  \item We provide a \textbf{detailed analysis of modality limitations,
        segmentation behaviour, and class-specific failure modes}, including
        an explicit comparison of every segmentation result against the
        degenerate all-foreground baseline, the complete classification
        collapse on Old Ice (F1\,=\,0.000), its statistical root cause, and a
        concrete fix path using calibrated dual-polarisation imagery.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%%====================================================================

\subsection{SAR-Based Sea Ice Analysis}

Sea ice characterisation from SAR data has a history spanning three decades.
Early work relied on statistical texture descriptors — co-occurrence matrices,
Gabor filters, and wavelet coefficients — combined with classical classifiers
such as support vector machines or random forests
\cite{brekke2005sea,leigh2014automated}. These pipelines are interpretable and
computationally lightweight but struggle to generalise across sensors,
incidence angles, and seasons.

The deep-learning wave reached SAR sea ice analysis around 2016--2020, with
convolutional networks demonstrating superior accuracy on standard benchmarks.
Liu et al. \cite{liu2021deep} trained a ResNet-based classifier on multi-temporal
Sentinel-1 scenes, while several groups adapted U-Net
\cite{ronneberger2015unet} to produce pixel-level sea ice concentration maps
\cite{wang2021sarunet}. A notable limitation shared by all of these efforts is
the exclusive focus on either segmentation \emph{or} classification. More
importantly, none incorporates natural-language conditioning — the image is
treated as a standalone visual signal, and the semantic richness of contextual
descriptions (acquisition time, region, expected ice type from prior knowledge)
is discarded.

\subsection{Multimodal Foundation Models for Segmentation}

The introduction of CLIP \cite{radford2021learning} marked a turning point. By
pre-training a ViT-L/14 image encoder \cite{dosovitskiy2021image} and a text
transformer on 400 million image--caption pairs with contrastive loss, CLIP
learned a joint embedding space in which semantically aligned images and texts
land close together. This property transfers surprisingly well to out-of-
distribution domains, even partially to SAR.

CLIPSeg \cite{luddecke2022image} leverages CLIP's shared embedding space for
zero-shot binary segmentation: a thin decoder conditioned on text embeddings
predicts a mask without requiring any pixel-level annotations at training time.
While this is an elegant generalisation, the absence of in-domain adaptation
limits performance on highly specific domains such as SAR sea ice.

More recently, Grounded SAM \cite{kirillov2023segment} chains a text-based
detection model with the Segment Anything Model (SAM) \cite{kirillov2023segment}
to produce open-vocabulary instance masks. Although powerful in natural-image
settings, such pipelines lack the fine-grained ice-type discrimination that our
task requires.

\subsection{Reasoning Segmentation and Multimodal Grounding Models}

Recent reasoning-segmentation systems couple an LLM decoder with a pixel-level
output head to enable instruction-following mask generation. LISA
\cite{lai2024lisa} inserts a special \texttt{[SEG]} token into LLaVA-7B's
vocabulary; when decoded, this token feeds a SAM-based mask head that produces
the corresponding region. LISA achieves strong results on natural images and
demonstrates genuine compositional reasoning from complex queries.

GeoPixel \cite{geopixel2024} extends this paradigm to remote sensing by
pretraining on geospatial imagery, adding SAM2-based mask refinement and
flash-attention-accelerated image encoding. Despite this domain specialisation,
GeoPixel's pretraining corpus consists predominantly of optical remote-sensing
imagery; its transfer to single-band SAR is limited by the modality gap.

While these systems demonstrate the value of language-instructed multimodal
understanding in general-domain and optical remote-sensing settings, their
applicability to SAR-based cryospheric monitoring remains largely unexplored.
The SAR modality introduces a fundamental domain gap: backscatter intensities
bear no direct resemblance to the photographic textures on which these models
were pretrained, and none of them has access to in-domain labelled data at
inference time.

A concurrent study on \emph{underwater} imagery \cite{underwater2024infofusion}
establishes the three-metric comparison protocol — gIoU, cIoU, and Dice — that
we adopt. Their results motivate our use of the same evaluation harness, making
direct method comparisons straightforward.

\subsection{Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning}

LoRA \cite{hu2021lora} addresses the cost of full fine-tuning by decomposing
weight updates into low-rank matrix products: $\Delta W = BA$ where
$B \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r}$, $A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k}$, and
$r \ll \min(d, k)$. Only $A$ and $B$ are trained; the original weight $W_0$
is frozen. Applied to the query, key, value, and output projection matrices of
a transformer, LoRA achieves performance competitive with full fine-tuning at
a fraction of the parameter cost. In our setting, four rank-8 LoRA adapters on
CLIP's attention projections account for 1.57 million trainable parameters
versus 304 million total — a 0.52\% ratio that keeps the adaptation tractable
even on a single GPU.

\section{Dataset}

\subsection{Image Corpus}

The dataset comprises 600 SAR images evenly distributed across six sea ice
categories (Table~\ref{tab:dataset}). Each image is a single-channel grayscale
JPEG of a SAR backscatter scene with a native resolution of
$256 \times 256$ pixels; images are bilinearly upsampled to
$512 \times 512$ at the input of the processing pipeline.
The six categories cover the full spectrum of Arctic sea ice life cycle and
morphology:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,label=\arabic*.]
  \item \textbf{Young Ice} — newly formed, thin ($<$30\,cm) ice with smooth surfaces and low backscatter.
  \item \textbf{First Year Ice} — intermediate-thickness ice that has survived one melt season; moderate backscatter variability.
  \item \textbf{Floating Ice} — buoyant, often fragmented ice masses with irregular shapes and mixed backscatter.
  \item \textbf{Glaciers} — land-based perennial ice bodies; distinct SAR signature owing to high surface roughness and bulk volume scattering.
  \item \textbf{Icebergs} — calved glacier fragments floating in open water; high contrast against surrounding sea surface.
  \item \textbf{Old (Multi-Year) Ice} — ice that has survived at least one melt season; physically thicker and rougher, but statistically indistinguishable from Young/First Year Ice in single-band intensity (see Section~\ref{sec:oi_failure}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Ground Truth: Derived Masks from Scattering Maps}
\label{sec:groundtruth}

For each image, a scattering map (stored as a second JPEG with filename suffix
\texttt{\_scat}) captures the spatial distribution of backscatter intensity.
These maps are continuous-valued, \emph{not} expert semantic annotations, and
their native resolution ($\approx 139 \times 187$, portrait) differs in both
size and aspect ratio from the square $256 \times 256$ images.
%% TODO: add \cite{} to the companion dataset paper (SeaIceReason) here and
%% state explicitly that its semantic annotation maps are NOT used in this work.

Binary ground-truth masks are derived at data-loading time in two steps.
First, per-image Otsu thresholding \cite{otsu1979threshold} is applied to the
scattering map at its native resolution, before any resampling, so that the
threshold is computed on the true intensity distribution. Second, the binary
mask is nearest-neighbour resized (non-uniformly stretched) to the image frame.
Because the aspect ratios differ, this stretch introduces a geometric
distortion of the label relative to the image content; we treat this as label
noise inherent to the dataset and discuss its consequences in
Section~\ref{sec:floor}. The same binarisation and resizing logic is applied
identically to all methods in the comparison harness, so no method gains an
evaluation-protocol advantage. The resulting labels are a \emph{relative}
threshold of each map's own intensity distribution — appropriate for
single-band SAR where absolute calibration is unavailable, but a coarse and
imperfect supervision signal, as we quantify with the all-foreground baseline
in Section~\ref{sec:mainresults}.

\subsection{Text Prompts and Leakage Control}
\label{sec:textprompts}

The dataset ships per-image natural-language descriptions (one to three
sentences recording acquisition context and visible ice features) in per-class
spreadsheet files. However, descriptions generated with knowledge of the class
are a potential leakage channel: even with the class name removed, text that
paraphrases class-defining physics could let the classifier read the label
from the prompt rather than the image.

The published configuration therefore uses a \textbf{fixed, class-agnostic
prompt for every image} — ``\emph{Identify and segment the most salient ice
region in this SAR image. Where are the strongest backscatter features?}'' —
encoded by CLIP's frozen text transformer and fused with the visual features.
Because the text input is identical for all images and all classes, it carries
zero class information, and the reported classification scores are by
construction attributable to the visual pathway alone. The text branch in this
configuration acts as a learned, constant conditioning signal rather than
per-image semantic guidance; exploiting the per-image descriptions without
re-introducing leakage (e.g., by scrubbing class-discriminative content) is
left to future work. The per-image descriptions are included in the released
dataset for that purpose.

\subsection{Overview}

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} illustrates the complete pipeline, which comprises
five sequential modules: (1) SAR preprocessing, (2) CLIP visual encoding with
LoRA adaptation, (3) cross-attention text--visual fusion, (4) U-Net segmentation
decoding, and (5) ice-type classification. Formally, given a SAR image
$I \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}$ and a text prompt
$\mathcal{T}$, the model outputs a binary mask
$\hat{M} \in \{0,1\}^{H \times W}$ and a class label
$\hat{y} \in \{0,\ldots,5\}$. No module generates language: the architecture
performs feature-level fusion of CLIP text embeddings with visual tokens.

\subsection{SAR Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preproc}

Raw SAR intensity values exhibit multiplicative speckle noise, which degrades
the statistical stability of downstream features. We first apply the Lee filter
\cite{lee1980digital} with a $7 \times 7$ window to suppress speckle while
preserving edge structures:

\begin{equation}
  \hat{I} = \mu_L + \frac{\sigma_L^2 - \mu_L^2 \, c_v^2}{\sigma_L^2}
             \left(I - \mu_L\right),
  \label{eq:lee}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mu_L$ and $\sigma_L^2$ are the local mean and variance over
the filter window, and $c_v$ is the coefficient of variation of the entire
image used as a noise estimate. The filtered intensity is then converted to
decibels:

\begin{equation}
  I_{\text{dB}} = 10 \log_{10}(\hat{I} + \epsilon),
  \label{eq:db}
\end{equation}

\noindent with $\epsilon = 10^{-6}$ for numerical stability. Finally, the
single-band result is replicated across three channels to form a pseudo-RGB
tensor $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 512 \times 512}$, which satisfies
the input contract of CLIP ViT-L/14 trained on natural RGB images. Channel-wise
mean and standard deviation normalisation using ImageNet statistics completes
the preprocessing step.

\subsection{Visual Encoder with LoRA Adaptation}
\label{sec:encoder}

We use the CLIP ViT-L/14 vision transformer as our visual backbone. The image
is divided into $16 \times 16$ non-overlapping patches, each projected to a
$d_v = 1024$-dimensional token. The frozen backbone therefore produces a
sequence $\mathbf{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 1024}$ of patch tokens, together
with attention weight matrices that we later use for auxiliary regularisation.

\paragraph{LoRA Adaptation.}
Rather than full fine-tuning of the 304-million-parameter backbone, we apply
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) \cite{hu2021lora} to the query (\textbf{Q}), key
(\textbf{K}), value (\textbf{V}), and output (\textbf{O}) projection matrices
of every self-attention layer. For a projection weight $\mathbf{W}_0 \in
\mathbb{R}^{d \times k}$, the adapted forward pass is:

\begin{equation}
  h = \mathbf{W}_0\,x + \mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}\,x,
  \quad \mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times r},\;
  \mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times k},\; r = 8,
  \label{eq:lora}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\mathbf{B}$ is initialised to zero and $\mathbf{A}$ to
random Gaussian, so that $\Delta\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{BA}$ starts at zero and
training recovers the correct direction. With four projection matrices per
attention block and 24 blocks in ViT-L/14, this yields approximately 1.57
million trainable parameters — 0.52\% of the total model size.

The text prompt $\mathcal{T}$ is encoded by CLIP's frozen text
transformer to produce a sentence embedding
$\mathbf{s} \in \mathbb{R}^{768}$ and token-level text features
$\mathbf{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{L \times 768}$, where $L$ is the number of
text tokens.

\subsection{Cross-Attention Text--Visual Fusion}
\label{sec:crossattn}

Raw patch tokens from ViT-L/14 have dimension 1024, while CLIP's text
transformer operates at 768 dimensions. A linear projection aligns visual
tokens to the text dimension before fusion. Four stacked cross-attention
blocks then integrate the two modalities:

\begin{equation}
  \text{CrossAttn}(\mathbf{Q}, \mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}) =
  \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)\mathbf{V},
  \label{eq:crossattn}
\end{equation}

\noindent where queries $\mathbf{Q}$ are derived from the visual patch tokens
and keys/values $\mathbf{K}, \mathbf{V}$ from the text token matrix
$\mathbf{T}$. This asymmetric formulation asks: ``given what I see in this
patch, which parts of the prompt are most relevant?'' Each block applies
multi-head attention (8 heads), layer normalisation, and a feed-forward
sublayer. The output is a fused token matrix
$\mathbf{F} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times 768}$ together with the sentence
embedding $\mathbf{s}$, both passed downstream.

\subsection{U-Net Segmentation Decoder}
\label{sec:decoder}

Pixel-level mask prediction requires spatial resolution recovery that
transformer encoders alone cannot provide. We adopt an image-conditioned U-Net
\cite{ronneberger2015unet} with four encoder--decoder scale levels. The fused
token sequence $\mathbf{F}$ is reshaped into a spatial feature map and
injected at the bottleneck of the U-Net via addition after a linear
projection, conditioning the decoder on the text-fused representation.

\paragraph{Auxiliary Deep Supervision.}
To stabilise gradients at shallow layers, we append a lightweight prediction
head at one-quarter resolution ($128 \times 128$) that produces an auxiliary
mask $\hat{M}_{\text{aux}}$. This head is discarded at inference; its sole
role is to propagate gradients deeper into the encoder during training.

\subsection{Ice-Type Classifier}
\label{sec:classifier}

Classification proceeds from a compact feature vector rather than from the full
spatial map. We form this vector by average-pooling the fused tokens
$\mathbf{F}$ over the spatial dimension, concatenating with the sentence
embedding $\mathbf{s}$, and passing through a two-layer MLP:

\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{z} = \text{MLP}\!\left(\left[\overline{\mathbf{F}} \,\|\, \mathbf{s}\right]\right),
  \quad \overline{\mathbf{F}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{F}_i,
  \label{eq:classifier_input}
\end{equation}

\noindent followed by a softmax over six classes. Masked pooling — restricting
the average to tokens within the predicted mask region — was also explored but
provided no consistent improvement and was not retained. We reiterate that the
prompt fed to the text branch is identical for every image
(Section~\ref{sec:textprompts}), so the classifier's input carries no textual
class information.

\subsection{Multi-Task Loss Function}
\label{sec:loss}

The full model is trained with four loss terms.

\paragraph{Tversky Loss.}
For these scattering-map targets, \emph{over}-detection is the dominant
failure mode: with a mean foreground fraction of roughly 38\%, an
all-foreground prediction is a stable minimum under objectives that penalise
missed ice more heavily than false alarms. The Tversky Index generalises the
Dice coefficient with asymmetric weighting \cite{salehi2017tversky}:

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{TI}(\alpha, \beta) =
  \frac{\text{TP}}{\text{TP} + \alpha\,\text{FP} + \beta\,\text{FN}},
  \quad \alpha + \beta = 1,
  \label{eq:tversky_index}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{Tversky}} = 1 - \mathcal{TI}(0.6,\, 0.4).
  \label{eq:tversky_loss}
\end{equation}

\noindent Setting $\alpha = 0.6 > \beta = 0.4$ penalises false positives more
than false negatives, deliberately pushing the operating point away from the
all-foreground degenerate solution and toward higher precision.

\paragraph{Focal Loss.}
To down-weight the contribution of easy, correctly classified background pixels
and concentrate learning on ambiguous boundaries, we include the Focal loss
\cite{lin2017focal}:

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}} =
  -\alpha_t \left(1 - p_t\right)^{\!\gamma} \log p_t,
  \quad \gamma = 2.
  \label{eq:focal}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Auxiliary Supervision.}
The auxiliary head at $\frac{1}{4}$ resolution is supervised with the same
Tversky loss applied to the downsampled ground truth
$y_{\text{aux}} \in \{0,1\}^{128 \times 128}$:

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{Tversky}}(\hat{M}_{\text{aux}},\, y_{\text{aux}}).
  \label{eq:aux_loss}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Classification Loss.}
Six-class classification is supervised with class-weighted cross-entropy
(weights $1.0/1.0/1.5/1.5/1.5/1.0$ for Young/First-Year/Floating/Glaciers/
Icebergs/Old):

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}} = -\sum_{c=0}^{5} w_c\, y_c \log \hat{y}_c.
  \label{eq:cls_loss}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Attention Regularisation.}
A small KL-divergence regulariser $\mathcal{L}_{\text{attn}}$ on the CLIP
attention maps discourages attention collapse. It contributes no supervision
signal beyond smoothing and carries a small weight.

\paragraph{Total Objective.}
The terms are combined as:

\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} =
  \lambda_{\text{mask}} \!\left(\mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{Tversky}}\right)
  + \lambda_{\text{aux}} \,\mathcal{L}_{\text{aux}}
  + \lambda_{\text{cls}} \,\mathcal{L}_{\text{cls}}
  + \lambda_{\text{attn}} \,\mathcal{L}_{\text{attn}},
  \label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\lambda_{\text{mask}} = 2.0$, $\lambda_{\text{aux}} = 0.2$,
$\lambda_{\text{cls}} = 0.5$, and $\lambda_{\text{attn}} = 0.05$, selected
empirically on the validation set. These values match the released training
configuration exactly.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

All experiments were conducted on a single NVIDIA A100 40\,GB GPU. The model
was trained for 50 epochs with the AdamW optimiser
\cite{loshchilov2019decoupled} ($\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$, weight decay
0.05) under a cosine annealing schedule with linear warmup over the first 5\%
of steps. Per-module learning rates follow the released configuration: LoRA
adapters $2\times10^{-4}$, U-Net decoder $2\times10^{-4}$, classification head
$1\times10^{-4}$, and fusion module $5\times10^{-5}$. The batch size was 4
with 4-step gradient accumulation (effective batch 16) and mixed-precision
arithmetic. Training stops early if validation mIoU fails to improve for 12
consecutive evaluations (not triggered in the final run). The CLIP backbone
was kept entirely frozen; only the LoRA parameters, the cross-attention fusion
module, the U-Net decoder, and the MLP classifier were updated. All
hyperparameters above are taken verbatim from \texttt{config.py} in the
released repository.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We report two complementary sets of metrics:

\paragraph{Segmentation (primary).}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{mIoU}: mean Intersection over Union, averaged over images.

  \item \textbf{cIoU}: cumulative IoU --- total intersection divided by total
        union over all pixels of the entire test set.

  \item \textbf{Dice}: mean per-image Dice coefficient
        $= 2\,\mathrm{TP}/(2\,\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{FN})$.

  \item \textbf{Pixel Accuracy}: fraction of correctly labelled pixels.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Classification (primary).}
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), leftmargin=*]
  \item \textbf{Weighted F1}: class-wise F1 averaged by support.

  \item \textbf{Accuracy}: fraction of correctly labelled images.

  \item \textbf{Macro F1}: unweighted class-wise F1 average.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Degenerate baselines.}
Because the Otsu-derived masks average roughly 38\% foreground, a trivial
predictor that labels \emph{every} pixel as ice already obtains a substantial
IoU. We therefore report the \textbf{all-foreground baseline} alongside every
segmentation result: its per-image IoU equals the ground-truth foreground
fraction, and on this test split it scores mIoU\,=\,0.383, cIoU\,=\,0.383,
Dice\,=\,0.480, and pixel accuracy 0.383 (precision 0.383, recall 1.0). For
classification, the corresponding floor is a uniform random classifier
(expected accuracy and F1 $= 1/6 \approx 0.167$ on this balanced test set).
The script \texttt{baseline\_allforeground.py} in the released repository
reproduces these numbers deterministically.

\paragraph{Comparison harness (gIoU / cIoU / Dice).}
For the multi-model comparison, every method is evaluated through the same
harness function that takes any predictor returning a binary mask for a given
image and outputs gIoU, cIoU, and Dice on the full 90-image test split. This
design eliminates differences in evaluation code as a confound.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), leftmargin=*]

\item \textbf{LoRA is critical for classification.} Removing LoRA reduces the weighted F1 score from 0.778 to 0.534 ($-0.244$), indicating that the low-rank adaptation layers learn ice-discriminative representations that are not captured by the frozen zero-shot CLIP embedding space.

\item \textbf{Tversky-only yields the strongest segmentation performance.} This configuration achieves the highest segmentation scores (mIoU\,=\,0.389, Dice\,=\,0.493), outperforming the full model on both metrics — though we note it clears the all-foreground floor (0.383 / 0.480) by only $+0.006$ mIoU and $+0.013$ Dice. The combined Focal--Tversky objective was retained in the final model because it provides a more balanced precision--recall trade-off, rather than maximising mIoU alone.

\item \textbf{Focal-only training leads to segmentation collapse.} When used in isolation, the Focal loss reduces segmentation performance substantially (mIoU\,=\,0.252). \item \textbf{The depth encoder provides no measurable benefit.} The ablation produces bit-identical results across all reported metrics, indicating that the DepthAnything V2 branch contributes no additional discriminative information. This finding is consistent with the absence of meaningful photometric depth cues in single-channel SAR imagery.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%%====================================================================

\subsection{Old Ice Classification Failure}
\label{sec:oi_failure}

The complete failure to distinguish Old (multi-year) ice (F1\,=\,0.000) is the
most prominent limitation of this work, and it is worth understanding precisely
why it occurs. Old ice is physically the \emph{opposite} of Young and
First-Year ice — thicker, mechanically weathered, with a more irregular surface
that generates higher volume backscatter in calibrated multi-polarisation SAR
\cite{ulaby1990microwave}. This separability holds in L-band and C-band
full-polarimetric imagery and in HV/HH polarisation ratio images.

In single-band HH or VV intensity JPEGs, however, that separability collapses.
Pixel-brightness statistics in our dataset bear this out quantitatively:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Class} & \textbf{Mean brightness} & \textbf{90th pct.} \\
\midrule
Old Ice       & 141.5 & 180.5 \\
Young Ice     & 140.8 & 178.4 \\
First Year Ice & 143.3 & 175.4 \\
\midrule
Glaciers      & 157.7 & 194.7 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Old Ice, Young Ice, and First Year Ice differ by less than 3 intensity counts
at both the mean and 90th-percentile level. Glaciers, which score F1\,=\,1.0,
sit 16 counts apart from this cluster at the mean. No classifier — linear or
nonlinear — can separate overlapping distributions without additional
discriminative signal. The fix is clear: calibrated dual-polarisation SAR
(HH and HV channels) or the derived polarimetric parameters (entropy,
anisotropy, alpha angle) would restore the physical separability that
single-band data discards.

\subsection{Proximity to the All-Foreground Floor}
\label{sec:floor}

The most consequential honest finding of this study concerns the segmentation
supervision. Because the Otsu-binarised scattering maps average roughly 38\%
foreground, the degenerate all-foreground predictor obtains mIoU\,=\,0.383 and
Dice\,=\,0.480 on the test split — values the full model does not exceed
(0.351 / 0.442) and the best ablated variant exceeds only marginally
(Tversky-only: 0.389 / 0.493).

Three factors compound to produce this ceiling. First, the supervision is a
per-image relative threshold of the same single-band intensity the model
receives as input; the labels contain no information the input does not.
Second, the scattering maps differ from the images in aspect ratio
($\approx$139$\times$187 vs.\ 256$\times$256), so the non-uniform stretch that
aligns them introduces geometric label noise that bounds achievable overlap.
Third, train and validation mIoU remain nearly identical throughout all 50
epochs — the model is not overfitting; it is saturating the information
available in the supervision.

The model's behaviour nonetheless differs qualitatively from the degenerate
solution: precision 0.444 against a 0.383 base rate, recall 0.640 rather than
1.0, pixel accuracy 0.656 against 0.383, and cumulative IoU 0.456 against
0.383. The network localises — it recovers substantial true background, which
an all-foreground predictor by definition cannot — but at this operating point
the per-image overlap with the noisy labels is no higher than the trivial
floor. We therefore present the segmentation track of this work as a
documented supervision ceiling and a calibrated harness for future work, not
as a performance claim. Breaking the ceiling requires better labels (expert
annotation or properly co-registered scattering maps) or richer inputs
(dual-polarisation channels), not a larger model.

\subsection{Comparison with Zero-Shot Baselines}
\label{sec:comparison_analysis}

The 53\% relative gIoU advantage of our model over LISA-7B (0.391 vs 0.255)
should be read carefully. LISA-7B operates zero-shot on SAR data it has never
encountered; our model has been trained on 420 in-domain images with per-pixel
supervision. This is an apples-to-oranges comparison in our favour. The correct
reading is \emph{not} that a 1.57M-parameter model is categorically superior to
a 7B-parameter one, but rather that (a) even a purpose-trained small model
meaningfully outperforms large models without domain exposure, and (b) there is
a large headroom to be filled by domain-adapted large models in future work.

GeoPixel's unexpectedly poor performance (0.032 gIoU) is notable: despite being
pretrained on optical remote-sensing imagery, it scores far below even CLIPSeg.
The likely cause is the SAR modality gap — GeoPixel's spatial priors are tuned
to photographic texture patterns that are entirely absent in SAR backscatter
maps, and the model's grounding mechanism may not produce meaningful
segmentation boundaries when the visual signal is unfamiliar. That all three
zero-shot systems fall below the all-foreground floor
(Table~\ref{tab:comparison}) makes the point sharper still: without domain
adaptation, current foundation models are not merely suboptimal on single-band
SAR — they are worse than a constant prediction.

We therefore wish to be explicit about how these comparisons should be read.
The primary contribution of this work is not demonstrating superiority over
general-purpose reasoning-segmentation models, but establishing a task-specific
baseline and highlighting the substantial domain gap between optical foundation
models and SAR imagery. The numbers in Table~\ref{tab:comparison} should be
interpreted as a measurement of that gap under a common evaluation protocol,
not as a claim that a compact in-domain model is intrinsically better than a
large multimodal one. We expect that domain-adapted large models, given
SAR-specific pretraining, would close and likely exceed this margin.

\subsection{Segmentation Precision--Recall Trade-off}

The ablation reveals a persistent tension between raw segmentation quality and
classification performance. The Tversky-Only variant maximises mIoU (0.389) and
Dice (0.493) but was not selected as the final configuration because the
Focal+Tversky combination yields a better-calibrated operating point for
downstream deployment. Precision 0.444 and Recall 0.640 for the full model
reflect a deliberate bias toward recall: missed ice is more dangerous for
navigation planning than marginal over-detection. This trade-off can be tuned
at inference via the binarisation threshold; Figure~\ref{fig:threshold}
illustrates sensitivity across the range $[0.2, 0.8]$.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{threshold.png}
  \caption{Threshold sensitivity on the 90-image test split. mIoU and Dice
           peak near 0.4, while the Precision--Recall crossover occurs around

\end{figure}

\subsection{Components Found Not to Contribute}

Three components present in the codebase — DepthAnything V2 depth encoding,
a temporal consistency module, and a SAM-based mask decoder — were evaluated
and excluded before the final model was locked. The depth encoder produced
bit-identical ablation metrics across two independent runs, confirming it adds
nothing on single-band imagery. The temporal module offered no measurable
benefit on per-scene evaluation (the dataset has no temporal sequences). The
SAM decoder was heavier than the U-Net alternative without producing better
masks, so it was replaced. These components remain in the repository for
reproducibility of their negative ablations. Likewise, the repository contains
optional BLIP-2 and LLaVA fusion backends; neither is used in any reported
result — the published model is the cross-attention configuration with no
language generation.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Directions}

The most immediate limitation is the single-band modality ceiling. Dual-
polarisation Sentinel-1 GRD products (HH + HV) or calibrated RADARSAT-2 SLC
data would provide the physical separability between Old Ice and its lookalikes.
A second direction is replacing per-image Otsu binarisation with expert-
annotated masks, which would simultaneously improve the quality of the
segmentation supervision signal, remove the aspect-ratio stretch noise
described in Section~\ref{sec:groundtruth}, and make per-image overlap metrics
meaningfully exceed the trivial floor for a competent model.

On the architecture side, the cross-attention fusion module currently operates
at the token level without explicit spatial awareness. Integrating position-
encoded multi-scale features (as in DINO \cite{caron2021emerging} or
Segment-Anything-2 \cite{ravi2024sam2}) could improve boundary localisation.
A further direction is exploiting the per-image descriptions shipped with the
dataset: the published model deliberately uses a constant prompt to preclude
label leakage (Section~\ref{sec:textprompts}), and designing a leakage-safe
protocol for genuinely informative per-image text is an open problem. More
broadly, domain-adapted reasoning-segmentation models — trained on SAR corpora
and equipped with polarimetric inputs — represent a natural evolution of this
work.

%%====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%%====================================================================

This work establishes, to the best of our knowledge, the first reported
deep-learning baseline for joint pixel-level segmentation and six-class
ice-type classification of sea ice from single-band SAR imagery, using a
language-conditioned multimodal architecture with 1.57\,M trainable
parameters. The results split cleanly into a positive and a negative finding,
and we report both.

The positive finding is classification: 0.833 accuracy and 0.778 weighted-F1
against a 0.167 chance floor, obtained with a constant text prompt that rules
out label leakage, with five of six ice types classified at F1\,$\geq$\,0.667.
The negative findings are equally informative: segmentation against
Otsu-binarised scattering maps does not exceed the all-foreground floor on
per-image overlap metrics (mIoU 0.351 vs.\ 0.383), a ceiling we trace to the
derived, geometrically distorted supervision rather than to the architecture;
Old Ice classification collapses entirely (F1\,=\,0.000) for reasons rooted in
the single-band modality; and all three zero-shot foundation-model baselines
(LISA-7B, CLIPSeg, GeoPixel-7B) fall below even the trivial floor,
quantifying the optical-to-SAR domain gap.

These results define exactly where the next generation of SAR sea-ice models
must invest: calibrated multi-polarisation inputs, expert-annotated or
properly co-registered labels, and leakage-safe protocols for informative
per-image text. We release the code, the evaluation harness, and the
degenerate-baseline script so that future work can be measured against the
same honest yardsticks.

We release all code and the evaluation harness at
\url{https://github.com/prakhar443/sea_ice_seg}; the trained checkpoint is
distributed through the repository's Releases page.
